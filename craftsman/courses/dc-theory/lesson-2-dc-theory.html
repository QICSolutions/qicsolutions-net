DC Theory

Understand how direct current behaves — so you can troubleshoot controls, batteries, and low-voltage systems with confidence.

⏱ 75 minutes ⚡ Apprentice level 🧮 Calculator + YouTube videos

Why DC Theory Is Lesson 2

After Safety First, DC theory is the foundation that makes every troubleshooting call click. Before you bend conduit or size conductors, you need to understand how voltage, current, and resistance interact.

DC (direct current) flows in one direction. You'll encounter it in control circuits, fire alarm systems, door access, solar DC wiring, battery backup, and vehicle electrical work. Master DC theory and the AC work ahead makes more sense too.

Learning Objectives

  • Define voltage, current, resistance, and power in plain language
  • Apply Ohm's Law to solve for any unknown quantity
  • Calculate power using P = V × I and its variations
  • Analyze simple series and parallel DC circuits
  • Connect DC theory to real field applications and troubleshooting

The Four Core Quantities

Every electrical calculation you'll ever do comes back to these four:

Voltage (V)

Electrical pressure that pushes current through a circuit. Measured in volts (V). Think of it as the "push" — a 12V battery pushes less than a 48V solar string.

Current (I)

The flow of electrons through a conductor. Measured in amperes (A). Current is what kills — respect it, even at low voltage.

Resistance (R)

Opposition to current flow. Measured in ohms (Ω). Every conductor and load has resistance — wire, resistors, coils, and connections.

Power (P)

The rate of energy transfer. Measured in watts (W). A 60W load at 12V draws more current than the same wattage at 120V.

Ohm's Law

The most important formula in electrical work:

V = I × R

Rearrange to solve for any variable:

Solve for Formula Example
CurrentI = V ÷ R12V ÷ 6Ω = 2A
ResistanceR = V ÷ I24V ÷ 4A = 6Ω
VoltageV = I × R3A × 8Ω = 24V
Field example: A 24V control circuit powers a relay coil rated at 120Ω. Current draw: I = 24 ÷ 120 = 0.2A (200 mA). Know this before sizing your power supply.

Electrical Power

Power tells you how much work the circuit is doing — and how much heat it generates:

P = V × I

Combine with Ohm's Law for two more forms you'll use constantly:

  • P = I² × R — useful when you know current and resistance
  • P = V² ÷ R — useful when you know voltage and resistance
Scenario Calculation Result
12V circuit, 2A drawP = 12 × 224 W
2A through 10Ω resistorP = 2² × 1040 W
48V across 96Ω loadP = 48² ÷ 9624 W

Series DC Circuits

Components connected end-to-end — one path for current:

  • Current is the same through every component
  • Voltage divides across each load proportionally to resistance
  • Total resistance adds: Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃...
Example: Three resistors in series — 4Ω, 6Ω, and 10Ω — on a 20V supply. Rtotal = 20Ω. Current: I = 20 ÷ 20 = 1A. Voltage across each: 4V, 6V, and 10V.

Parallel DC Circuits

Components connected across the same two points — multiple current paths:

  • Voltage is the same across every branch
  • Current divides among branches inversely to resistance
  • Total resistance: 1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃...
Example: Two 12Ω resistors in parallel on a 24V supply. Rtotal = 6Ω. Total current: I = 24 ÷ 6 = 4A (2A per branch).

Most branch circuits in buildings are parallel — each outlet and fixture gets the same voltage independently.

Key Takeaways

01

DC Theory is Lesson 2 — the theoretical foundation right after Safety First.

02

V = I × R. Know it forward, backward, and in your sleep.

03

Power = V × I. Higher current at the same voltage means more watts and more heat.

04

Series: resistance adds, current stays constant. Parallel: voltage stays constant, resistance divides.

Controls, alarms, solar, and batteries all run on DC — this lesson applies tomorrow on the job.
