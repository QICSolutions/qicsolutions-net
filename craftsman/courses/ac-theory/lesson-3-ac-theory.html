AC Theory

Alternating current powers every building you wire — understand the waveform, the math, and the real-world impact on loads.

⏱ 90 minutes 〰️ Apprentice level 🧮 Calculator + YouTube videos

Why AC Theory Is Lesson 3

You learned DC Theory in Lesson 2 — voltage, current, and resistance in one direction. Now you need AC (alternating current), because that's what comes out of every breaker, feeds every motor, and defines how you size conductors and overcurrent protection.

AC isn't just DC that changes direction. Phase relationships, reactance, and power factor all affect how circuits behave in the real world. This lesson gives you the foundation to understand those effects before you dive into NEC load calculations and motor circuits.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how AC differs from DC and why the utility grid uses AC
  • Describe sine waves, frequency, and RMS voltage values
  • Distinguish real, apparent, and reactive power — and calculate power factor
  • Understand basic inductive and capacitive reactance in AC circuits
  • Compare single-phase and three-phase power distribution

AC vs DC

In a DC circuit, current flows in one steady direction. In an AC circuit, current reverses direction at a fixed rate — in North America, 60 times per second (60 Hz).

DC

Constant polarity. Batteries, control circuits, solar DC strings, and electronics. Ohm's Law applies directly: V = I × R.

AC

Reversing polarity. Utility power, branch circuits, motors, transformers. Requires RMS values and power factor for accurate calculations.

Why AC won the grid:

  • Transformers work with AC — easy voltage step-up for transmission, step-down for distribution
  • Lower losses at high voltage over long distances
  • Motors are simpler on AC — induction motors dominate industry

Waveforms & RMS Values

Standard US utility power is a sine wave at 60 Hz — one complete cycle every 1/60th of a second.

Vpeak = VRMS × √2 ≈ VRMS × 1.414

Common RMS Voltage Peak Voltage Application
120V~170VResidential branch circuits (line to neutral)
208V~294VCommercial three-phase (line to line)
240V~339VResidential split-phase (line to line)
277V~392VCommercial lighting (line to neutral on 480V system)
480V~679VCommercial three-phase (line to line)
Why RMS matters: Your multimeter reads RMS. A "120V" outlet delivers 120V RMS — not peak. Peak voltage is what determines insulation stress. Always know which value you're working with.

AC Power — Real, Apparent, and Reactive

In AC circuits with inductive or capacitive loads, voltage and current are not always in phase. That changes how power is calculated:

Type Symbol Unit Formula
Real power (true power)PWatts (W)P = V × I × cos θ
Apparent powerSVolt-Amps (VA)S = V × I
Reactive powerQVARQ = V × I × sin θ

Power Factor (PF) = P ÷ S = cos θ

  • PF = 1.0 — purely resistive load (incandescent heater, resistive element). Voltage and current are in phase.
  • PF = 0.7–0.9 — typical induction motor under load. Some current does "reactive" work magnetizing the motor.
  • Low PF — utility and large facilities care because low PF means more current for the same real power, increasing losses.
Field example: A 120V motor drawing 10A with PF = 0.85. Real power: P = 120 × 10 × 0.85 = 1,020 W. Apparent power: S = 120 × 10 = 1,200 VA. You're billed on real power (kWh), but conductors and breakers must handle the full 10A.

Reactance & Impedance

AC circuits include opposition beyond pure resistance:

Inductive Reactance (XL)

Opposition from inductors, motor windings, and ballasts. Increases with frequency. Causes current to lag voltage.

Capacitive Reactance (XC)

Opposition from capacitors and power factor correction equipment. Causes current to lead voltage.

Impedance (Z) = √(R² + X²)

For apprentice-level troubleshooting, remember: motors add inductance, capacitors correct power factor, and your clamp meter reads current based on impedance — not just resistance.

Single-Phase vs Three-Phase

Single-Phase

One AC waveform. Standard in US homes — 120V line-to-neutral, 240V line-to-line from a center-tapped transformer. Powers lighting, receptacles, and small appliances.

Three-Phase

Three AC waveforms, 120° apart. Standard in commercial and industrial. Delivers more power with less copper for the same voltage class.

System Line-to-Neutral Line-to-Line Common Use
120/240V Split-Phase120V240VResidential
120/208V Wye120V208VCommercial (small)
277/480V Wye277V480VCommercial (large)
Remember: √3 (≈ 1.732) appears in three-phase calculations. Line-to-line voltage = line-to-neutral × √3 on wye systems. You'll use this in load calculations later.

AC in the Field

Where AC theory shows up on your next job:

  • Sizing breakers and conductors — based on full current draw (apparent power), not just real power
  • Motor startups — inrush current can be 5–8× FLA; AC phase relationships affect this
  • Voltage drop — AC impedance (not just DC resistance) affects long runs
  • Harmonics — non-linear loads (VFDs, LED drivers) distort the sine wave; know when to escalate
  • Power factor correction — capacitors on large motor circuits to improve PF and reduce utility penalties

Key Takeaways

01

AC reverses direction at 60 Hz in the US — it's what powers every branch circuit and motor.

02

Multimeters read RMS. Peak voltage = RMS × √2. A 120V outlet peaks at ~170V.

03

Real power (W) = V × I × PF. Breakers and wire size must handle full current, not just real power.

04

Motors add inductance and lower power factor. Capacitors correct PF on large installations.

05

Residential is single-phase. Commercial is typically three-phase — know your line and line-to-line voltages.
